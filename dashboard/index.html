Habeas Protocol

A computational layer for three operating commercial tribunals — the DIFC Courts in Dubai, the ADGM Courts in Abu Dhabi, and the Singapore International Commercial Court (SICC). Updated April 28, 2026.

Start here

What this is, in plain words

The Habeas Protocol is a software layer for international commercial courts. When two businesses in different countries have a contract dispute — say a Dubai-based fintech and a UK supplier — they take it to a specialised commercial court like DIFC (Dubai), ADGM (Abu Dhabi), or SICC (Singapore). Those courts publish their rules and their judgments, but they live in PDFs. There is no way today for software to ask "what would this court do with this case?" This dashboard demonstrates a working answer to that question.

Below you will find 121 real judgments from those three courts, each scored against six properties a digital tribunal needs to satisfy. Twelve real legal rules from those judgments have been encoded in Catala, an open-source programming language designed for law. Seven worked examples reproduce real courts' real arithmetic on real fact patterns. You can run the rules yourself, draft new ones, and route hypothetical cross-border cases — without needing to read a line of legalese.

The bet of this project is the opposite of most "Web3 court" proposals: the courts already exist and are excellent. What is missing is the computational layer.

06 — Cross-border
Route a multi-jurisdiction dispute →

Pick a local forum, an originating forum (if a foreign judgment is in play), and a claim type. The router returns the rule chain — recognition gates first, then substantive rules.

How to read the page below

  1. At a glance — headline numbers: judgment count per tribunal, mean scores, working traces.
  2. The Atlas — every judgment, one sigil each. Filter by tribunal; click a sigil for the full record. Same scores produce the same fingerprint, so duplicates and outliers are visible at a glance.
  3. The six primitives — the rubric every ruling is scored against. Each primitive is a Yes / Partial / No question about the judgment's structure.
  4. Heatmap + bar chart — how each tribunal scores on each primitive, then a side-by-side mean comparison.
  5. System properties — two architectural checks (separation of powers, enforceability) at the tribunal level rather than per-ruling.
  6. Working traces — seven worked examples. Each is a rule + a fact pattern + the calculation. Click "view trace" to step through.
  7. All judgments — searchable, sortable table of the full corpus.

Glossary — quick definitions

Tribunal
A court that hears a particular kind of dispute. DIFC, ADGM, and SICC are the three commercial tribunals coded in this corpus.
Primitive
One of six properties — for example "rule clearly stated", "evidence dated", "calculation reproducible" — that we score every judgment on. The full rubric is in data/primitives.json.
System property
An architectural check at the tribunal level — separation of powers, enforceability under the New York Convention. Not per-ruling.
Catala
An open-source programming language designed for encoding legal rules. It compiles prose-and-formula source into a typechecked, executable predicate.
Trace
A worked example: rule + fact pattern + step-by-step output that reproduces a real judgment's calculation.
Rule library
The 12 Catala modules under rules/. Each one encodes a specific legal rule from a specific tribunal.
Certification
The lifecycle a rule passes through (draft → submitted → reviewed → certified) before it is safe to rely on. Defined in rules/_certification.yaml.
Conflict of laws
The rules that decide which jurisdiction's law applies to a cross-border dispute, and which court hears it.
Coded vs raw
"Coded" judgments have structured primitive scores; "raw" judgments are scraped court documents that are searchable but not yet scored.

If something looks broken

The dashboard talks to a local API server. If a section says "API unavailable" or a control is greyed out, the API server is not running. From the repo root: python3 api/server.py. The dashboard reaches it at http://127.0.0.1:5544. The static pages still work without the API — only interactive pages (Playground, Simulator, Authoring, Ingestion, Cross-border) need it.

Empirical findings

All three tribunals score at near-ceiling on every per-ruling primitive, and 2/2 on both architectural system properties. The pattern is robust to two stress tests, and four executable traces from the corpus demonstrate the protocol's methodological coverage.

Of the 121 judgments scored, 39 form a hand-coded gold set (32 DIFC + 7 ADGM); the remaining 82 entries (69 ADGM + 13 SICC) are AI-triaged and AI-graded against the same rubric, with per-entry provenance recorded in the coding.coder field.

  • Sample-size robustness. The ADGM saturation pattern survives a 10× expansion: mean primitive score drifts from 1.93 at n=7 (hand-coded) to 1.91 at n=76 (hand + AI). The near-ceiling reading isn't an artefact of small-sample noise.
  • Cross-family replication. SICC at 1.95 / 2.00 replicates the saturation in a third operating tribunal in a different legal family — Singapore common law via the IAA, vs DIFC's own statutes and ADGM's English-law-via-statute. The protocol is not court-specific.
  • Architectural system properties. All three tribunals score 2/2 on separation of powers (SP1) and enforceability under the New York Convention (SP2). These are the structural pre-conditions for plugging software into the bench.
  • Methodological coverage. Four executable traces from the corpus show the protocol covers (i) static-rule arithmetic, (ii) deferred conditionals, (iii) rule-bounded human judgment, and (iv) arithmetic composition over substantive findings. Three further traces extend to Boolean composition over contractual interpretation, NY-Convention partial refusal under Singapore IAA s 31, and a third-party-jurisdiction gate under Norwich Pharmacal + Bankers Trust + RDC 28.52.

The tribunals already exist; what is missing is the computational layer.

How to read a fingerprint

Six concentric rings encode the per-ruling primitives, innermost to outermost: PR1 rule source · PR2 typed evidence · PR3 machine-readable order · PR4 procedural state · PR5 reasoning trace · PR6 replayability.

A full ring with eight tick-marks means a perfect score (2). A half-arc with four ticks means a partial score (1). A faint dashed circle means absent (0).

Two outer arcs encode the system properties: SP1 separation of powers (top), SP2 appeal path (bottom). The central rosette is a hash-seeded ornament unique to the case ID — so two judgments with identical scores still wear different faces. A small dot at the upper-right marks one of the four cases that became an executable trace.

Tribunal comparison: mean primitive scores

Mean score per primitive, with DIFC and ADGM side-by-side. ADGM scores at or near ceiling on every per-ruling primitive (1.96 average across six). DIFC averages 1.73, weakest on the Enforcement Bridge primitive — where ADGM wins because ADGM judgments explicitly bind to UAE federal law via Cabinet Resolution.

Finding: Two operating tribunals, both implementing the full protocol at near-ceiling, available to plug in today. The Maxim thesis does not require building a tribunal from scratch; it requires layering computation on top of the ones that already exist.

Seven working traces

Each trace lifts a rule from the corpus into Catala source plus a Python predicate evaluator and runs it against an event log of the case facts. The seven traces span the full methodological spectrum the protocol claims — formula, deferred conditional, bounded discretion, arithmetic composition over substantive findings, Boolean composition over contractual-interpretation findings, partial statutory refusal of NY Convention enforcement under Singapore IAA s 31, and a third-party-jurisdiction gate (Norwich Pharmacal + Bankers Trust + RDC 28.52) over a digital-asset tracing dispute in DIFC's Digital Economy Court. Three tribunals (DIFC + ADGM + SICC) and three legal families (DIFC own statutes, ADGM English-law-via-statute, Singapore IAA + NY Convention) all run through the same predicate engine. Trace #3 is the methodologically necessary one: it shows what the rules cannot fully decide.
